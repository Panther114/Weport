# 02 — Academic State of the Art: Making an LLM Talk Like One Specific Real Person

**Scope.** This document surveys the *academic* literature relevant to building a faithful **imitation** of one specific person's informal chat style from that person's own messages (WeChat, Chinese/English code-mixed, ~104,000 messages, 2026-03 → 2026-09, mostly 2–3 sentences or shorter per message). It is the inverse of authorship *attribution*: instead of asking "who wrote this?", it asks "how do I make the model write like *him*?", and "how would I know if I succeeded?".

**Verification policy.** Every claim below carries a URL. Numbers are quoted as reported by the source. Anything I could not confirm against a primary source is explicitly marked **UNVERIFIED**, including named-but-unfound work. Where a source is a non-peer-reviewed artifact (blog, GitHub README, preprint), that is stated. Search-result snippets were used only where marked; the load-bearing numbers come from papers/pages I opened.

**Bias of the evidence base.** Almost all high-quality stylometry evidence comes from *literary* or *long-form* text (novels, plays, essays, emails). Chat is the hardest case in the literature and the one with the least evidence. Two facts should frame everything that follows:

1. The single closest study to this product — **Wang et al., "Catch Me If You Can? Not Yet" (Findings of EMNLP 2025)** — concludes that in-context imitation works *partially* on structured genres (news, email) and **"struggles with nuanced, informal writing in blogs and forums"** ([arXiv:2509.14543](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.14543v1), [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.532/)).
2. In Abbasi & Chen's Writeprints study — the canonical "identify a person from online text" paper — the **chat** dataset was by far the weakest: 50.4% / 42.6% / 31.7% accuracy at 25 / 50 / 100 authors, versus 92–96% on email and eBay comments ([Abbasi & Chen 2008, ACM TOIS 26(2)](https://ahmedabbasi.com/wp-content/uploads/J/AbbasiChen_Writeprints_ACMTOIS.pdf)).

Both say the same thing: **chat style is the frontier, not the solved case.** The owner's complaint that the clone "doesn't know how to actually speak" is consistent with the published state of the art, not an implementation defect alone.

## 1. Stylometry / authorship attribution: which features actually discriminate

### 1.1 The classical results, with their numbers

| Study | Method | Headline number |
|---|---|---|
| **Mosteller & Wallace 1963/64** — *Inference in an Authorship Problem*, JASA 58(301):275–309 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500849), [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/bayes/1963-mosteller.pdf)) | Bayesian + discriminant analysis on **function-word rates** ("filler words … articles, prepositions, conjunctions provide fairly stable rates, whereas more meaningful words like *war*, *executive*, *legislature* do not") | Concluded **Madison wrote all 12 disputed Federalist papers**. `upon` was the single most important discriminator "by a factor of 4", followed by `whilst`, `there`, `on`. Hamilton calibrating mean +0.92, Madison −0.38, midpoint 0.27, **no overlap** between the known-author sets. Sentence length was useless — Hamilton 34.55 vs Madison 34.59 words, SD 19.2/20.3, i.e. "practically twins" |
| **Burrows 2002** — *'Delta': a measure of stylistic difference and a guide to likely authorship*, LLC 17(3):267–287 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/17.3.267)) | z-scored relative frequencies of the **150 most frequent words**, mean absolute deviation from an author's mean profile | "comparatively accurate … for texts exceeding about **1,500 words**"; "of even greater value as a method of reducing the field of likely candidates for texts of as little as **100 words**" |
| **Hoover 2004** — *Testing Burrows's Delta*, LLC 19(4):453–475 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/19.4.453)) | systematic variation of Delta | **"much larger numbers of frequent words are even more accurate than the 150 that Burrows tested"**; **removing personal pronouns** and words for which a single text supplies most occurrences **greatly increases accuracy**; combining several texts per author reduces intra-author variability |
| **Smith & Aldridge 2011** — *Improving Authorship Attribution: Optimizing Burrows' Delta Method*, JQL 18(1) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09296174.2011.533591)) | feature-count + distance sweep | **A word vector of 200–300 words gives the most accurate results**; adapting Delta to **cosine similarity gives "a dramatic improvement in accuracy"** (this is "Cosine Delta" / "Würzburg Delta") |
| **Jannidis/Evert et al. 2015** — *Towards a better understanding of Burrows's Delta*, ACL workshop ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3115/v1/w15-0709), summarised in [Evert et al. 2017 DSH](https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqx023)) | 14 Delta variants, 3 corpora, clustering quality | **Cosine Delta (Δ∠) consistently outperforms all other Delta variants regardless of the number of features**, stays robust up to **n_w = 10,000**; **vector normalisation is the key factor** behind its success and also improves classic Delta |
| **Eder 2010** — *Does Size Matter? Authorship Attribution, Small Samples, Big Problem*, DH2010 ([PDF](https://dh2010.cch.kcl.ac.uk/academic-programme/abstracts/papers/pdf/ab-744.pdf)) | controlled length sweep, 500→20,000-word samples, 63 English novels + 8 more corpora | **"samples shorter than 5,000 words provide a poor 'guessing'… below 3,000 words the obtained results are simply disastrous"**; critical point **5,000–10,000 words**; poetry 3,500; Latin prose 2,500; the *shape* of the curve is method-independent |
| **Luyckx & Daelemans 2011** — *The effect of author set size and data size in authorship attribution*, LLC ([PDF](https://www.clips.uantwerpen.be/~walter/papers/2011/ld11.pdf)) | learning curves on 3 corpora | Cites **Burrows 2007: 10,000 words per author is "a reliable minimum for an authorial set"**; **Sanderson & Guenter 2006: 5,000 words of training is a minimum requirement**; the PERSONAE corpus has ~**1,400 words/author** and 145-way attribution reaches only **10%** accuracy (vs 3% with one 140-word fragment/author); AAAC_A 13-way: **27% → 50%** (char trigrams) from 10% → 90% of training data; **character n-grams are the best feature type at every data size tested** |
| **Koppel, Schler & Argamon 2009** — *Computational methods in authorship attribution*, JASIST 60(1):9–26 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.20961), [PDF](https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~koppel/papers/authorship-JASIST-final.pdf)) | feature × learner grid on emails (488 msgs, "some as short as a single word"), 9 novelists (500-word chunks), 20 bloggers | **SVM and Bayesian regression far superior** to other learners; **character trigrams perform almost identically to content words and significantly outperform them on blogs**, capturing "acronyms and abbreviations characteristic of blog writing"; naive Bayes "performs very poorly for all feature sets" |
| **Abbasi & Chen 2008** — *Writeprints*, ACM TOIS 26(2) Art. 7 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1344411.1344413)) | lexical + syntactic + structural + content-specific + idiosyncratic features, K-L transforms with per-author feature sets | **as high as 94% when differentiating between 100 authors**; **individual-author-level feature sets beat a single group-level set** (p<0.01 on Enron and CyberWatch); Enron email 92.0/90.4/83.1%, eBay comments 96.0/95.2/91.3%, Java forum 88.8/66.4/52.7%, **CyberWatch chat 50.4/42.6/31.7% (25/50/100 authors)** |
| **Grieve 2007** — *Quantitative authorship attribution* ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqm020), [thesis PDF](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/8840/etd1721.pdf)) | 39 textual measurements on one dataset | No single measurement wins; **"the best approach … involves the analysis of many different types of textual measurement"** (word/sentence length, vocabulary richness, graphemes, word frequencies, punctuation, positional stylometry, n-grams) |
| **Baayen, van Halteren, Neijt & Tweedie 2002** — *An experiment in authorship attribution*, JADT ([PDF](https://quantling.org/~hbaayen/publications/BaayenVanHalterenNeijtTweedieJADT2002.pdf)) | controlled topic/genre; 8 authors writing on identical topics | Plain LDA on function words was **at chance**; entropy-weighted LDA (ELDA) with **60 function words reached 81.5%**; controlling topic/genre imbalance alone added ~10 points; and — directly relevant here — **"we were surprised by the extent to which the simple inclusion of punctuation marks in the analysis enhanced classification accuracy"** |

**Reading of the classics for this project.** Two of them cut against lazy intuitions:
- **Function words are not the whole story in informal text.** The 2025 PLOS ONE ALM study explicitly finds "**content words classes are characterized by a higher density of authorship information than function word classes, challenging a long-standing assumption of stylometry**" ([Huang et al. 2025, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0327081)).
- **Bigrams and higher word n-grams are often *worse* than unigrams on short informal text.** In Hinglish WhatsApp data, word bigrams dropped accuracy to ~60–68% while word **unigrams and character 3-grams** reached ~95% ([arXiv:1812.10281](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.10281)).

### 1.3 Cheapest locally + most discriminative (ranked)

For a local desktop app that must compute features over ~104k messages in milliseconds-to-seconds, ranked by (evidence strength × cheapness):

1. **Character 3–5-gram frequency vectors** (hashing into a fixed-size vector). Cheapest possible (one pass, no tokenizer, no dictionary), works on unsegmented code-mixed text, and is the single most consistently top-performing family on *informal short* text. This is the one feature family the literature recommends when you cannot afford anything else.
2. **Function-word / stopword-vector + Cosine Delta** (or plain cosine) over ~200–500 MFW. Cheap; the classical workhorse; needs a Chinese+English stopword list and message-boundary-aware tokenisation.
3. **Punctuation/whitespace profile** (counts of `。，！？~…、!!, ???, 2333, hhh, 233, ……`, half-width vs full-width, whether a period is used at all, trailing-space habits). Effectively free, and both Baayen et al. and the WhatsApp profiling study found it strongly discriminative in unedited text.
4. **Emoji / sticker / emoticon usage vector** (rate per message, type distribution, position in message — initial/middle/final/alone). Highest measured lift on short informal text (65→85%); cheap given an emoji table.
5. **Idiosyncratic token list** (typos, pinyin abbreviations, homophone substitutions, pet words, laughter spellings). Highest measured accuracy on tweets (98.5%) but requires per-user extraction — which is exactly what you have 104k messages for.
6. **Message-length and burst-length distributions** (chars/message, messages/burst, burst duration). Free; 92–96% alone on tweets; also the feature that makes splitting behaviour imitable.
7. **Per-author LM perplexity (a small local LM fine-tuned on the corpus)** — moves from "feature" to "metric/model"; see §3.6.
8. **Syntactic parse features** — strong in the literature but *expensive and fragile on code-mixed chat*; deprioritise.

**Implication.** A *single* 2–3-sentence WeChat message is a ~10–40 token document. Every classical threshold says: do not try to score style per message. **Score style over aggregates** (a recipient's whole thread, a week of messages, a burst), and use per-message features only for cheap *lexical* checks (catchphrase rate, emoji rate, punctuation signature). Fortunately the corpus is ~104k messages: if each is ~10–40 tokens that is roughly **1–4M tokens of one person**, i.e. two to three orders of magnitude above every "reliable minimum" in the table.

### 1.5 Chat / micro-text specifically — what the results actually are

- **Schwartz et al. 2013, "Authorship Attribution of Micro-Messages" (EMNLP)** — the canonical short-text attribution paper. Introduces author "k-signatures" (features present in ≥k% of one author's training samples and no one else's) and "flexible patterns" (the context in which function words occur). Results: **50 authors / 50 training tweets → 50.7%; 50 authors / 1,000 tweets → 71.2%; 1,000 authors / 200 tweets → 30.3%** (random baseline 0.1%); flexible patterns add **6.1% over the previous state of the art**; word n-grams add ~3% over character n-grams alone. ([ACL D13-1193](https://aclanthology.org/D13-1193.pdf))
- **Forensic microblog analysis** (arXiv:2003.11545): 40 users, 120–200 tweets each. **Idiosyncratic features 98.5% > char n-grams 97% > lexical 92–96% ≥ structural 92–96%**. The paper's own summary: "such idiolectic/idiosyncratic features are the ones providing the highest accuracy".
- **Hinglish WhatsApp** (arXiv:1812.10281): 4 authors, ~76,000 words, code-mixed Hindi-English in Latin script — the closest published analogue to code-mixed chat. **SVM + char 3-grams: 95.079%**; word unigrams 94.862%; **binary weighting beat TF and TF-IDF**; word bigrams collapsed to ~60–68%.
- **WhatsApp N-gram tracing for profiling** (KIMLI 2025, DOI 10.51817/kimli.v2025i.145): ~2.1M tokens of WhatsApp + microblog; SVM/Random Forest **82–93%** attribution accuracy. Most predictive: **function words, character sequences reflecting spelling choices, and punctuation patterns**, plus "abbreviations, code-switching, and unique styles present in informal communication". Character-level n-grams beat word-level "particularly in short or informal texts where word-level features may be sparse or noisy".
- **WeChat-adjacent emoji study** (Nature *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 2025, [link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05464-w)): 40 Chinese participants, 3 years of natural WeChat conversations. Emoji/text ratio **4.42% (younger) vs 5.99% (older)**; emoji overwhelmingly appear **at the end of a turn or alone**, rarely at the beginning or middle; people concentrate on a small set of emojis that dominate their usage; intimacy with the recipient changes emoji diversity (more diverse with intimates, more restricted with distant contacts). Annotation reliability Cohen's κ = 0.853.
- **Interpersonal emoji study** (IEEE IJCIME 2019, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/ijcime49369.2019.00059)): 514 one-to-one chat corpora from 1,028 interlocutors. Same findings plus: with casual acquaintances, users are **less** likely to use emoji to carry meaning and **more** likely to use explicit words — i.e. **register genuinely changes per recipient**.

### 1.6 One author is not one distribution

This is the single most under-appreciated finding for the WeClone design:

- **Rexha et al.** (*Scientometrics* 2018, [PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11192-018-2661-6.pdf)): human annotators asked to rank snippets by style similarity to a source snippet had **Krippendorff's α = 0.299**, observed disagreement 0.699; precision of picking the true same-author snippet was **near the 25% random baseline** — even with 56 crowd annotators. The task "turns out to be very challenging".
- **Wegmann et al. 2022** (*Same Author or Just Same Topic?*, RepL4NLP, [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2022.repl4nlp-1.26/)): authorship-verification-trained style representations **leak content**; controlling for conversation is what makes representations style- rather than topic-driven.
- **Eder 2010**: Delta "works least well with texts of a genre uncharacteristic of their author" (as Burrows put it) — style is genre-conditional.

Practical translation: a single "style vector" for the user is a *coarse* summary. The literature supports **per-recipient (or per-register) style models** rather than one global profile — which is exactly what "audience design" predicts (§5.4) and what the WeChat emoji studies measure.

## 2. Text style transfer / authorial style imitation with LLMs

### 2.1 The direct evidence: can LLMs imitate *this person*?

**Wang, Tripto, Park, Li & Zhou 2025 — "Catch Me If You Can? Not Yet: LLMs Still Struggle to Imitate the Implicit Writing Styles of Everyday Authors", Findings of EMNLP 2025, pp. 10040–10055** ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.532/), [arXiv:2509.14543](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.14543v1), [code](https://github.com/jaaack-wang/llms-implicit-writing-styles-imitation)). This is the paper the WeClone design should be built around.

- **Design**: in-context imitation from a *few* user-authored samples, **no explicit stylistic instructions**, conditioned on a content summary. **>40,000 generations per model**; **>400 real authors**; four domains: Enron emails, Blogs, CCAT50 news, Reddit.
- **Four complementary evaluators**, which is the template for a fidelity harness: (1) an **authorship-attribution (AA)** model trained on human texts, (2) an **authorship-verification (AV)** model applied to (generation, human reference) pairs, (3) a per-author **style model** built from **LIWC + WritePrint (Abbasi & Chen 2008)** feature sets with **Mahalanobis distance**, (4) **GPTZero** as an AI-detection/human-likeness proxy.
- **Framework validation on human text** (upper bound of the evaluators): AV accuracy **87.7–91.4%**; AA top-5 **79.8–95.5%**; style-model matching **69.1–81.9%**; human texts almost never flagged as AI. Datasets: Enron 150 authors / 3,884 samples / avg length 309; Blog 100 / 25,224 / 319; CCAT50 50 / 2,500 / 584; Reddit 100 / 8,451 / 333.
- **Headline finding**: "**LLMs can approximate user styles in structured formats like news and email, they struggle with nuanced, informal writing in blogs and forums. Generated outputs often default to an average, generic tone and remain readily detectable as AI-written.**" Increasing the number of demonstrations gives **limited** gains.
- **Exact AV numbers** for LLM generations (5-shot vs 0-shot), CCAT50: GPT-4o 95.28 vs 93.14; GPT-4o-mini 95.16 vs 93.38; Gemini-2.0-Flash 97.44 vs 93.94; Gemma-3-27B 97.34 vs 94.18; DeepSeek-V3 97.46 vs —; Llama-4-Maverick 94.68 vs —. Performance is **lower on Reddit and Blog**.
- **AI-detection proxy**: "the percentage of LLM-generated texts classified as human-written is … **always below 55% and often less than 20%**".
- **Few-shot > zero-shot is significant** (Wilcoxon signed-rank on Mahalanobis distances to the author's style model), and **zero-shot already beats random AA** because of topical overlap — a warning that AA-style metrics partly measure *content*, not style.

**Jemama & Kumar 2025 — "How Well Do LLMs Imitate Human Writing Style?", IEEE UEMCON 2025** ([arXiv:2509.24930](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24930)). A training-free verifier (TF-IDF char n-grams + transformer embeddings, classified by empirical distance distributions) reaching **97.49% accuracy / AUC 0.997 / F1 0.975 in-domain** and **94.48% / AUC 0.981 cross-domain**, with 91.8% less training time and 59% less memory than parameter-based baselines. Then five LLMs (Llama, Qwen, Mixtral) × four prompting strategies:

- **Zero-shot fails completely: accuracy below 7%** — "the statistical style summaries provided in prompts were not effective anchors for imitation".
- **One-shot improves drastically but variably: 67.6%–94.7%.**
- **Few-shot reaches near-perfect style matching: up to 23.5× higher style-matching accuracy than zero-shot.**
- **Text completion reaches 99.9%** agreement — "once a stylistic context is provided, continuation tasks may evade detection by stylometric means".
- **Fidelity ≠ detectability**: human essays average **perplexity 29.5**, matched LLM outputs only **15.2** (another passage reports 16.07); at a perplexity threshold of ≤20, **~90% of AI outputs fall below it vs ~15% of human texts**.

**The two papers agree on the mechanism**: (a) exemplars matter enormously, (b) prompt-level statistical summaries ("here is a description of my style") are close to worthless, (c) informal/chat register is the weakest case, (d) even a stylometrically "perfect" imitation remains statistically detectable because it is too *predictable*.

### 2.2 Role-play and persona benchmarks (what they measure, what they don't)

**What this literature does and does not give you.** It gives you *evaluation paradigms* (personality back-testing, interrogation, capability decomposition, hallucination rate) and *Chinese-language data*. It does **not** give you a recipe for imitating a *real, non-celebrity, living* person from their own chat logs — with the partial exception of TwinVoice's Interpersonal dimension.

### 2.3 Style embeddings and style transfer methods

- **StyleDistance** (Patel et al., NAACL 2025, [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.436/), [arXiv:2410.12757](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12757)): trains content-independent style embeddings using **LLM-generated near-exact paraphrases with controlled style variation across 40 distinct style features** (SynthStel: 100 positive/negative pairs per feature). Beats prior style representations on **STEL** and the harder **STEL-or-Content** benchmark; a model trained *only* on synthetic data generalises to natural text. Releases `StyleDistance/styledistance` embeddings — **directly usable as a local fidelity metric** (cosine between the clone's message and the user's real messages, in a style-only space).
- **Wegmann, Schraagen & Nguyen 2022** (RepL4NLP, [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2022.repl4nlp-1.26/)): the underlying STEL framework; representations trained with *conversation* control are better at style-over-content than domain control or no control.
- **Rivera-Soto et al. 2021** (EMNLP, [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.70/)): **authorship representations are not universal** — transfer between domains is uneven; Reddit-trained models transfer best (topic diversity + size); the data must be controlled to learn the desired invariance. Consequence: a general "author embedding" will not reliably capture a WeChat/Chinese-English idiolect.
- **TINYSTYLER** (Findings of EMNLP 2024, [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.781.pdf)): an 800M-parameter T5 conditioned on a **mean-pooled authorship embedding** of N target examples performs few-shot style transfer "on par with state-of-the-art LLMs" at a fraction of the cost; evaluated with **NUM EXAMPLES = 16 and 64**; distilled on 40k self-generated pairs. This is the strongest argument that **a small local model + a style vector can substitute for a large prompted model**.
- **StyleRemix** (EMNLP 2024, [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.241/)): interpretable obfuscation by perturbing specific style axes with **per-axis LoRA modules**; releases **AuthorMix** (30k long-form texts, 14 authors, 4 domains) and **DiSC** (1,500 parallel texts, 7 style axes, 16 directions). Confirms that *axis-wise* control beats one monolithic "style" knob.
- **Masks and Mimicry: Strategic Obfuscation and Impersonation Attacks on Authorship Verification** (NLP4DH 2025, [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2025.nlp4dh-1.10.pdf)): against a strong AV model (BigBird), **obfuscation attack success rate up to 92%**, **impersonation (targeted style transfer) up to 78%**, using zero-shot Mistral prompting and DIPPER paraphrasing plus a **RAG pipeline that retrieves style properties from the target author's prior writings with chain-of-thought prompting**. Two lessons: (1) LLM-driven style imitation is strong enough to fool a state-of-the-art verifier, and (2) the *attack* recipe — retrieve the target's own prior texts, extract style properties, then rewrite — is structurally the same as what a good clone should do.
- **LLM-based text style transfer survey** (IEEE Access 2025, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/access.2025.3548967)) groups methods into **prompting, fine-tuning, and memory-augmented LLMs** — i.e. the same three levers available locally.

### 2.4 Personalisation by fine-tuning and by steering

- **Panza** (Nicolicioiu et al., [arXiv:2407.10994](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10994), v4 Feb 2025) — *the* fully-local personalised-writing study: email generation, **Reverse Instructions** to turn each user's emails into (instruction → original email) pairs, then fine-tune, optionally with RAG. Key numbers: **"very little data — under 100 email samples — are sufficient to create models that convincingly imitate humans"**; at a training set of **25 examples, BLEU is already at ~90% of its maximum and MAUVE ≈ 0.85**; **all fine-tuning regimes beat "pretrained + RAG" by a large margin**, and base LLMs with RAG "largely fail to match a given user's writing style"; **RoSA ≈ full fine-tuning > LoRA**, especially on MAUVE; runs in <24 GB, merges into ~15 GB for inference, and is feasible on a free Colab tier.
- **OPPU — One PEFT Per User** (Tan et al., EMNLP 2024, [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.372/), [arXiv:2402.04401](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.04401)): per-user LoRA modules over seven LaMP tasks; **parametric (PEFT) + non-parametric (retrieval + profile) beats either alone** (+1.93%/+2.48% from retrieval; +4.56%/+7.18% from profiles); PEFT copes better than retrieval when the history is *not* highly relevant to the query — the "user behaviour shift" case.
- **LaMP benchmark** (Salemi et al., [arXiv:2304.11406](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.11406)): 7 personalised tasks; retrieval-augmented personalisation gives **+12.2% relative zero-shot, +23.5% with fine-tuning**. Retrievers compared: BM25, Contriever, Recency, Random.
- **CARD** (arXiv:2601.06352): cluster-level LoRA adapters + a per-user **128-dim preference vector injected only at decoding**, base model frozen, adapters stored on-device. Group LoRA improves LLM-judge scores by **66.7% / 50.0% / 56.4%** on LaMP-4/5/7 and human-eval win rates by **72.7% / 94.4% / 70.0%** — while ROUGE gains stay modest, i.e. **lexical-overlap metrics under-report style personalisation**.
- **In-Dialogue Learning (IDL)** (EMNLP 2024, [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.581.pdf)): fine-tunes LLaMA-2 to infer persona **from dialogue history alone, with no pre-defined profile**; BLEU +200%, ROUGE +247%; human evaluation: IDL beats ICL with win rates **68.8% (persona)** and **59.0% (style)**; annotator agreement κ 0.53/0.56/0.51; and notably **ICL "underperforms in personalized dialogue generation … it fails to effectively utilize persona information within these dialogues"**. This is the strongest published argument that **profiles-plus-ICL is the weak configuration** for personal dialogue.
- **Activation steering / style vectors**: `CAS` contrastive activation steering derives a per-user **style vector** by contrasting the user's authentic responses with style-neutral LLM responses ([SteerX, arXiv:2510.22256](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.22256)); **Steerable Chatbots** (arXiv:2505.04260) user study (n=14) finds activation-steering control aligns better with latent preferences than prompting alone in cold-start personalisation. **Persona vectors** (Anthropic, [arXiv:2507.21509](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.21509), [blog](https://www.anthropic.com/news/persona-vectors)) show traits are **linear directions** that can be monitored and steered, and that **training-data projection onto a persona vector predicts post-fine-tuning personality shift** — a practical pre-flight check on a style-training set.
- **RoleCraft-GLM** (Personalize 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.personalize-1.1)): 27,259 multi-turn dialogues across **20 non-celebrity Chinese personas**, each utterance emotion-labelled; emotion annotations improved role-specific knowledge memory (Rouge-L 0.4641 vs 0.4570 baseline, SPE 0.3544 vs 0.3406).

### 2.5 Persona drift and stability

- **Measuring and Controlling Persona Drift in Language Model Dialogs** (arXiv:2402.10962, [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10962v1)): self-chat protocol with **100 persona system prompts in 5 categories**, each with a probe question and a programmatic persona measure. **LLaMA2-chat-70B shows "significant persona drift within eight rounds of conversations."** Diagnosis: **attention decay** across turns (`π(t)` constant within a turn, falling across turns). Mitigation: **split-softmax**, a training-free, parameter-free attention re-scaling of system-prompt tokens, which beats prompt repetition and a prior method on the stability/MMLU-trade-off curve.
- **Persona Drift Detection in Role-Playing Agents** (ICASSP 2026, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp55912.2026.11463024)): three-dimension framework (hard facts, soft facts, **stylistic features**) with **Time-to-First-Drift (TtFD)**, evaluated over **100-round** simulations across ChatGPT, Qwen, Gemini, DeepSeek.
- **PICon** (arXiv:2603.25620), above: even with a **context reset**, persona agents are inconsistently stable; greedy decoding with shuffled question order does **not** fix it.
- **CORE / PERSIST** (arXiv:2609.12373): separates turn-local evidence from persistent persona-state revision with uncertainty-aware belief revision; a post-anchor benchmark for persona-state robustness under ambiguity, conflict and social influence.

**Why this matters for WeClone**: drift within 8 turns means a persona-clone conversation longer than a handful of exchanges will silently revert to assistant register unless the persona is re-injected (system prompt repetition, split-softmax-style attention re-weighting, or per-turn re-retrieval). Per-turn retrieval (which WeClone already does) is a partial mitigation because it re-injects style tokens *late* in the context; the drift literature suggests deliberately placing the strongest style exemplars **immediately before the generation point**, not only at the top of the prompt.

## 3. Metrics that can actually be computed to score style fidelity

This section is the operational core: what to compute, the formula, a reference implementation, and — critically — whether it survives at 2–3 sentence message length.

### 3.1 Distance metrics over style vectors

**(a) Burrows's Classic Delta.** Take the *n* most frequent words in the corpus (MFW), compute each text's relative frequency for each word, **z-score each word's frequency across the corpus** (subtract corpus mean, divide by corpus standard deviation), then the distance between two texts is the **mean of the absolute differences of their z-scores**:

```
Δ(T1, T2) = (1/n) · Σ_{i=1..n} | z_i(T1) − z_i(T2) |,   z_i(T) = (f_i(T) − μ_i) / σ_i
```

Defaults in Eder, Rybicki & Kestemont's `stylo` R package: `mfw.min = mfw.max = 100`, `culling = 0`, `distance.measure = "delta"`, `start.at = 1` — verified in the [package source](https://github.com/computationalstylistics/stylo/blob/master/R/stylo.default.settings.R). The same file warns that "Euclidean Distance and Manhattan Distance should be avoided in stylometry", and notes Canberra is "risky, but sometimes amazingly good". Culling is defined as the percentage of texts in a corpus in which a word must appear to be retained (`culling = 0` ⇒ no culling; `100%` ⇒ the word must appear in every text).

**(b) Cosine Delta (Δ∠, "Würzburg Delta").** Same z-scored MFW vectors, but **L2-normalise each vector** and take the cosine distance. Jannidis/Evert et al. found it dominates every other variant and stays robust to n_w up to 10,000 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3115/v1/w15-0709), [Evert et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqx023)); Smith & Aldridge found **200–300 words optimal** for the classic form and called the cosine switch "a dramatic improvement" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09296174.2011.533591)). **This is the right default local metric.**

**(c) Cosine similarity over function-word vectors** — the same thing without z-scoring; cheaper, weaker. Use it as a fast pre-filter.

**Length caveat (measured).** The 2025 EMNLP Findings study found that **even the zero-shot LLM outputs achieved non-trivial authorship-attribution accuracy "largely driven by the topical/content overlap"** — i.e. an attribution-style metric applied to short generations is partly measuring *content*. Any Delta/AA metric must therefore be run on **content-matched** pairs (see §3.5) or reported alongside a content-only baseline.

### 3.2 Distributional divergence over n-gram distributions

- **KL divergence** `D_KL(P‖Q) = Σ P(x) log(P(x)/Q(x))` is asymmetric and undefined where `Q(x) = 0` — you must smooth (add-α or Krichevsky–Trofimov). MAUVE's own implementation notes it uses KT smoothing in the `mauve_star` variant ([MAUVE docs](https://krishnap25.github.io/mauve/)).
- **Jensen–Shannon divergence** `JSD(P,Q) = ½D_KL(P‖M) + ½D_KL(Q‖M)`, `M = ½(P+Q)` — symmetric, bounded in [0, log 2] (nats) / [0,1] if base-2, and always finite (no zero-probability blow-up). **JSD is the right choice for a local clone metric**; report `√JSD` (Jensen–Shannon *distance*) so it is a metric.

The 2026 paper *From Context Shift to Stylistic Collapse* uses exactly this family as "distributional probes" over 24 linguistically-motivated features and reports divergence in percent terms, which shows the method is usable at corpus scale ([arXiv:2605.28826](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28826)). I found **no canonical stylometry paper that establishes JSD-over-n-grams as *the* style metric** — treat JSD as a sound information-theoretic tool with strong indirect support, not as a cited standard. **UNVERIFIED as a stylometric standard.**

### 3.3 Lexical diversity (compute, but interpret carefully)

- **TTR** = V/N (types/tokens). **Known to be invalid across different lengths** — "if the TTR is calculated for different length samples of a single text … its value will continue to fall as more yet-to-be-seen word-types are introduced" ([Grieve thesis](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/8840/etd1721.pdf)).
- **MATTR** (moving-average TTR): TTR computed over a sliding window of fixed length (typically 50–100 tokens) then averaged. Length-robust by construction because every window has the same N.
- **MTLD** (McCarthy & Jarvis 2010): "the mean length of sequential word strings that maintain a given TTR value (here, **.720**)"; you walk the token stream, accumulate a "factor" until TTR drops to 0.720, reset, and average the factor lengths. Reported as **"the only index not found to vary as a function of text length"** among MTLD, vocd-D, HD-D, TTR, Maas, Yule's K ([validation study](https://scispace.com/pdf/mtld-vocd-d-and-hd-d-a-validation-study-of-sophisticated-3j1xvu4x33.pdf)). Implementation: `lexicalrichness` (Python), `koRpus`/`qdap` (R). Requires ≥ ~100 tokens to be meaningful at all — **for a 20-token chat message it is noise**.
- **Yule's K** (Yule 1944): `K = 10⁴ · (Σ_m m² V_m − N) / N²` where `V_m` is the number of types occurring exactly `m` times and `N` the token count. Theoretically length-invariant, but Tweedie & Baayen showed **"nearly all available measures are highly dependent on text length"** in real text, and recommended using only **Z(N)** and **K(N)** as the two useful families (richness and repeat rate) ([Tweedie & Baayen 1998](https://quantling.org/~hbaayen/publications/TweedieBaayen1998.pdf)).
- Verdict for a 20-token message: **all lexical-diversity metrics are noise.** Use them only on **per-recipient thread aggregates of ≥ 500–1,000 tokens**, and prefer MTLD + MATTR over TTR and Yule's K.

### 3.4 Compression-based distance (the cheapest serious metric)

**NCD** `= (C(xy) − min(C(x), C(y))) / max(C(x), C(y))`, where `C(·)` is the compressed size in bytes. **CCC** (conditional complexity of compression) is the alternative `C(xy) − C(x)`. Evidence ([Comparing compression models for authorship attribution, FSI 2013](https://www.inf.ufpr.br/lesoliveira/download/FSI2013.pdf)):

- **NCD works well instance-based; CCC works well profile-based.** "Compressing all the references before computing the similarity makes the NCD not suitable at all for the profile-based approach", whereas CCC + BZip reached **98% recognition** profile-based.
- Compressor choice matters profile-based: **GZip clearly worse** than BZip/PPM (GZip's 32 KB sliding window means "the beginning of that document will be ignored"), and Lempel-Ziv dictionaries are a poor fit for long profiles.
- Reported: GZip-NCD **99%** vs **82%** for stylometric features + SVM on the same protocol.
- Python: `zlib.compress` / `gzip` in the stdlib; `ncd` packages exist but are trivial to implement. **Minimum length**: NCD needs enough bytes for the compressor to find structure; on a ~50-byte Chinese chat message the compressed sizes are dominated by header/entropy-coding overhead and NCD is **unreliable — treat as UNVERIFIED at that scale**. Use NCD on aggregates (a whole thread, a day of messages).

### 3.5 The authorship-verification framing (the most defensible fidelity score)

This is what the best 2025 evaluation used. Train a same-author/different-author verifier on the user's real messages using **impostor pairs** (real messages split into two halves vs. messages paired with other people's messages), then score the clone by the **fraction of generated messages accepted as same-author, and by ROC-AUC**.

- Reported verifier quality on human text (the ceiling): AV accuracy **87.7–91.4%**, AA top-5 **79.8–95.5%** ([Wang et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.532/)).
- **Practical protocol (from the same paper, plus the classical impostor design):**
  1. Hold out ~10% of the user's messages (stratified by recipient and by month).
  2. Train/obtain a verifier (a fine-tuned transformer, or the training-free TF-IDF-char-n-gram + embedding scheme of [Jemama & Kumar](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24930), which reports AUC 0.997 in-domain — suspiciously high, so re-validate on your own data).
  3. Generate clone messages **for the same content** as the held-out ones (e.g. ask the clone to reply to the same incoming message, or to convey the same summary).
  4. Report **% accepted as same-author** and the **ROC-AUC** over the pair distribution, plus a **content-only control** (a generic LLM given the same content) to prove the metric is measuring style, not topic.
- **Why this matters**: a same-author verifier is the *inverse task* of the product. It is the only metric family in the literature that has been shown to actually move when style imitation improves, and it is exactly where LLM impersonation attacks succeed (up to **78% attack success** against a strong BigBird verifier, [NLP4DH 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.nlp4dh-1.10.pdf)).

### 3.6 Per-author language-model perplexity (best single metric for a local app)

**ALMs — Authorial Language Models** (Huang et al.; [arXiv:2401.12005](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.12005v2.pdf), journal version in [PLOS ONE 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0327081)): fine-tune one causal LM per candidate author; attribute a text to the author whose model gives it the **lowest perplexity** (`ppl = exp(cross-entropy)`).

- **83.6% macro-accuracy on Blogs50 (SOTA), 74.9% on CCAT50.**
- **Ablation by length**: to reach **70% macro-accuracy ALMs needs 40 tokens on Blogs50** and 400 tokens on CCAT50; to reach **60%** it needs **20 tokens on Blogs50** and 70 tokens on CCAT50. Blogs50 is the informal, personal-writer corpus — i.e. **on informal text, 20–40 tokens is enough for a per-author LM to discriminate**.
- The follow-up analysis finds **content-word classes carry more authorship information than function-word classes** — a direct challenge to the classic assumption.

**How to use locally**: fine-tune one **small** LM (a 0.3–1.5B model, or even a character-level n-gram LM as a cheap proxy) on the user's ~1–4M tokens. Score a generated message as `ppl_user(msg)` and compare to the distribution of `ppl_user` over held-out real messages (z-score). Also compute `ppl_base(msg)` under the untuned base model: the **gap** is a style-fit signal that is robust to topic. Note the danger flagged by UEMCON: LLM output is systematically *too predictable* (perplexity 15–16 vs 29.5 for humans), so a clone that scores "too well" on user-perplexity is probably regurgitating.

### 3.7 Distribution-level metrics — MAUVE, and why BERTScore is the wrong tool

- **MAUVE** (Pillutla et al., NeurIPS 2021 Outstanding Paper; [site](https://krishnap25.github.io/mauve/), [code](https://github.com/krishnap25/mauve)): the area under the divergence frontier between two text distributions in a **quantised embedding space**, i.e. a KL-based gap between the model distribution and human distribution. Crucially: "**each distribution must contain at least a few thousand samples (we use 5000 each). MAUVE with a smaller number of samples is biased towards optimism** … and exhibits a larger standard deviation between runs." Also: **only relative comparisons are meaningful**, absolute values depend on hyperparameters. → **Not usable per-message; usable only as a periodic corpus-level regression check** (e.g. 5,000 clone messages vs 5,000 real messages, same topic mix).
- **Why MAUVE still earns a place**: Panza used MAUVE as a headline personalisation metric and found **all fine-tuning regimes beat pretrained+RAG**, with **RoSA consistently surpassing LoRA "especially in terms of MAUVE score"** ([arXiv:2407.10994](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10994)). It is a real, previously-used signal for this exact task.
- **BERTScore is the wrong metric here.** BERTScore embeds reference and candidate with a contextual encoder and matches tokens by cosine similarity — it is a *semantic* metric. The fine-grained analysis at WMT 2021 found BERTScore "**is less sensitive to smaller errors, especially if the candidate is lexically or stylistically similar to the reference**", and it "fails to assign low scores when a bad candidate … has high lexical overlap with the reference in terms of **content words**"; it "is more easily fooled when the difference is only in **function (not content) words**" ([ACL WMT 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.wmt-1.59/)). Function words and punctuation are the *core* of style — so BERTScore is systematically blind to exactly what this product is trying to fix. It will reward a fluent generic paraphrase that preserves meaning. **Do not use BERTScore as the primary clone metric.** (Same objection applies to BLEU/ROUGE; CARD's results confirm that lexical-overlap metrics show "relatively modest" gains where human and LLM-judge evaluations show 50–94% improvements, [arXiv:2601.06352](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06352).)

### 3.8 Perplexity, bits-per-character and "detectability"

Two distinct uses:
1. **Fit**: perplexity under the author-specific LM (§3.6).
2. **Human-likeness**: perplexity under a *general* LM. Real informal human text has *higher* perplexity than LLM output. Reported: **human essays 29.5 vs matched LLM outputs 15.2**; at a threshold of 20, ~90% of AI outputs vs ~15% of human texts fall below ([arXiv:2509.24930](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24930)). WeChat messages are short and slangy, so absolute numbers will differ, but the **direction is the useful signal**: a clone whose output is systematically *more* predictable than the user's real messages is not imitating — it is smoothing.

### 3.9 Human evaluation: how the literature runs it, and what it reports

- **Forced-choice style similarity / authorship discrimination**: Rexha et al. 2018 gave 56 crowd annotators one source snippet and four candidates (one same-author) and measured **Krippendorff's α = 0.299** with precision near the 25% random baseline — **humans are bad at this and disagree heavily**.
- **Chat-style TST**: LMStyle Benchmark ([arXiv:2403.08943](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08943)) formalises two dimensions — **style strength** and a novel **appropriateness** (coherence + fluency + implicitness) — and finds its **NLL-based appropriateness score has the highest correlation with human judgement** (Pearson and Kendall), ChatGPT-scoring second, SBERT third, and **BLEU performing poorly, sometimes negatively correlated**. Its style-strength classifier correlates only ~25% with human style-strength ratings.
- **Inter-rater reliability for style-transfer evaluation** (Mir et al., NAACL 2019, [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/N19-1049.pdf)): content-preservation κ = **0.297** (style-masked) vs **0.173** (unmasked) — masking style words improves reliability; style-transfer-intensity relative scoring did **not** beat absolute scoring; relative scoring did improve naturalness agreement. Their recommended automated metrics are **direction-corrected Earth Mover's Distance** for intensity and **Word Mover's Distance on style-masked text** for preservation.
- **Persona dialogue human eval** (IDL, EMNLP 2024): 5 annotators, 500 sampled dialogues, **Win/Tie/Lose** per aspect; κ = 0.53 (persona), 0.56 (style), 0.51 (fluency).
- **Human detection of AI text is unreliable — use it only as a soft signal**: 187 participants averaged **57% accurate** on human-vs-AI texts and **78%** on social-media comments ([Scientific Reports 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-76218-y)); a separate study of AI vs human short stories found participants **at or below chance** ([Bot or not](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/45E6DC0BB90AA648654D5AE243F6C667/S1930297526100424a.pdf)).

### 3.10 Which metrics survive at 2–3 sentence message length?

| Metric | Per-message (10–40 tokens)? | Aggregate (thread / week, ≥500 tokens)? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Character n-gram + cosine | **Weak but usable as a ranking signal** (char n-grams are the one family that works on short text) | **Yes — strongest cheap option** | Use 3–5 grams, binary or TF weighting |
| Cosine Delta / MFW cosine | **Noisy** — MFW frequencies are sparse below ~500 words | **Yes** | Needs 200–300 MFW, z-scored |
| JSD / KL over char n-gram distributions | **No** (sampling noise dominates) | **Yes** | JS with KT/α smoothing |
| MTLD / MATTR / TTR / Yule's K | **No — pure noise** | **Yes** (MTLD, MATTR preferred) | Length-dependence is proven |
| NCD (gzip) | **No** | **Yes** | Use CCC/BZip for profile comparison |
| AV model (same-author probability) | **Yes, if the verifier is trained on this person's own message lengths** | Yes | Report % accepted + AUC; include a content-only control |
| Per-author LM perplexity | **Yes for this corpus** — 20 tokens suffices on informal text (Blogs50) | Yes | The single best "does this sound like him" scalar |
| Punctuation / emoji / typo-rate deltas | **Yes** — these are per-message quantities by nature | Yes | Report as rates with confidence intervals |
| Perplexity under a general LM (detectability) | Yes (noisy) | Yes | Expect clone ≪ human perplexity if generic |
| MAUVE | **No — needs thousands of samples** | Yes (5,000+ each side) | Relative comparisons only |
| BERTScore / BLEU / ROUGE | **No — wrong construct** | No | Rewards fluent generic text |
| Human A/B | Yes (and the only ground truth) | Yes | Low agreement is the norm; use many raters and forced choice |
